# AI and Youth Employment in Bhutan

**To:** Director, Department of Employment and Entrepreneurship, Ministry of Industry, Commerce and Employment (MoICE)

**Re:** Implementing Bhutan’s National AI Strategy 2025: Managing AI’s Impact on Youth Employment

## Executive summary

Bhutan’s youth unemployment was 19.0% in 2024, far above the 3.5% overall unemployment rate (National Statistics Bureau Bhutan 2025). As Bhutan’s National AI Strategy 2025 supports AI adoption in tourism and public-service delivery (Royal Government of Bhutan, GovTech Agency 2025), routine entry-level tasks may be automated faster than new work pathways are created. However, AI exposure does not automatically mean job loss. International Labour Organization research indicates that AI is more likely to transform tasks and occupations than eliminate entire jobs (International Labour Organization 2025b). This brief recommends a two-year AI-Augmented Youth Pathways Package: foundational AI literacy, sector-specific training linked to paid placements and conditional employer incentives combined with responsible public procurement. Indicative cost: Nu 95–125 million.

## The policy problem

Bhutan has a serious school-to-work transition problem. In 2024, overall unemployment was 3.5%, while youth unemployment was 19.0%. In Q4 2025, youth unemployment reached 20.6%, compared with 3.8% overall (National Statistics Bureau Bhutan 2026). The gap shows that young people face barriers beyond a general lack of vacancies, including limited work experience, skills mismatch and employer reluctance to recruit inexperienced workers.

AI can intensify these barriers. Firms adopt AI when it lowers the cost or time of completing tasks, improves quality or expands output. In tourism, finance and public administration, AI can automate routine enquiries, information processing and standard documents.

If AI replaces junior labour, the tasks that serve as entry points disappear, deepening youth unemployment even as productivity rises.

However, this outcome is not inevitable. AI is more likely to reorganise tasks than eliminate whole occupations. Workers remain necessary for judgement, accountability, cultural understanding, relationship-building, handling exceptions and checking AI outputs (International Labour Organization 2025b). The policy issue is whether adoption is designed to augment young workers or substitute for them.

## Labour-market impacts

### Tasks and employment

Across tourism, finance and public administration, workers will shift from routine processing toward judgement, verification and exception handling. This can raise productivity because workers complete standard tasks faster and focus on higher-value activities.

However, productivity does not automatically create jobs. Employers may use AI to expand output and services, increasing employment, or to produce the same output with fewer workers. The outcome depends on demand, business strategy and whether workers have skills for redesigned roles.

ILO evidence identifies clerical support work as highly exposed to generative AI automation, making entry-level pathways particularly important for young jobseekers (International Labour Organization 2025b).

### Wages and job quality

AI augmentation can improve wages if it raises productivity and gives workers higher-value responsibilities. AI-skilled workers may become more valuable to employers. However, if AI reduces junior positions, greater competition for entry-level jobs may weaken starting wages and conditions. Benefits may be captured by firms and already skilled workers unless young people receive recognised training, workplace experience and pathways to progress (International Labour Organization 2025a).

### Distribution and industry structure

AI’s effects will not be equal. Women may be disproportionately exposed because clerical and administrative occupations have high AI exposure and women are often more represented in these roles (International Labour Organization 2025b). Rural youth, persons with disabilities and people without reliable devices or connectivity risk exclusion if training is online-only or concentrated in urban areas.

Larger organisations may adopt AI faster than smaller tourism businesses. This can increase industry concentration and reduce local employment opportunities unless smaller firms receive adoption support.

## Policy options

### Option 1: National AI literacy program

MoICE would provide free short courses covering AI basics, prompt writing, data privacy and digital safety.

The target group is young people aged 18–29, including students and jobseekers. Delivery should combine online learning with regional access through schools, employment centres and training hubs. Indicative cost: Nu 10–15 million over two years.

This option is cheap, scalable and quick to implement. Its weakness is that AI awareness alone does not create jobs or work experience. It may also exclude young people without devices or internet access.

### Option 2: AI-Augmented Youth Skills and Placement Program

MoICE would fund employer-designed short courses in AI-supported service delivery and AI-output verification, linked to paid placements.

The program would support approximately 1,000 unemployed or underemployed people aged 18–29, prioritising women, rural youth and recent graduates. Indicative cost: Nu 50–70 million over two years.

This option addresses the experience gap. Young people gain relevant skills, a recognised credential, a mentor and paid workplace experience. Employers gain candidates prepared for AI-enabled work. Its main limitation is that it requires strong employer and provider coordination.

### Option 3: Youth AI Employment Incentive and Responsible Procurement Standard

MoICE would offer a temporary wage subsidy of about Nu 60,000 per placement to employers that create new, supervised AI-enabled entry-level roles. Employers would provide training, a named mentor, acceptable wages and confirmation that the role is additional.

Government agencies purchasing AI would complete a workforce-impact assessment covering affected tasks, retraining, privacy risks and youth apprenticeship opportunities. This promotes responsible AI adoption.

The scheme would target approximately 500 placements in tourism, finance, digital services and selected public-sector roles. Indicative cost: Nu 35–40 million over two years.

This option can create jobs quickly. Its main risk is deadweight, where firms receive subsidies for jobs they would have created anyway. Strong eligibility checks and audits are necessary.

## Evaluation and recommendation

The options should be judged against efficiency, equity, feasibility and fiscal cost.

| Criterion | Option 1: Literacy | Option 2: Skills and placements | Option 3: Incentives and procurement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Efficiency | Low cost but weak employment effect | Strong link between skills, experience and employer demand | Can create jobs quickly but deadweight reduces value |
| Equity | Moderate if regional access is funded | High if targeted recruitment and paid placements are enforced | Moderate, depends on employer participation and compliance |
| Feasibility | High | Moderate | Moderate |
| Cost | Nu 10–15m | Nu 50–70m | Nu 35–40m |

MoICE should adopt a combined AI-Augmented Youth Pathways Package. It combines broad access through literacy, stronger employment pathways through placements, and employer incentives conditional on job quality. The indicative cost is Nu 95–125 million over two years and should be refined with employers, providers and the Ministry of Finance.

## Implementation and KPIs

**Months 1–3:** MoICE establishes a steering group with the GovTech Agency, training institutions, tourism and finance employers, youth representatives and worker representatives. The group maps AI-exposed entry-level tasks and identifies pilot districts.

**Months 4–6:** MoICE develops courses, selects providers, publishes subsidy and audit rules, and introduces a workforce-impact template for public AI procurement.

**Months 7–12:** Launch AI literacy modules, recruit the first cohort, and begin training, placements and employer incentives.

**Year 2:** Expand to further districts if results are positive, audit employers, publish an interim report and revise the program. Risks, including weak employer engagement, low-quality placements, rural exclusion and subsidy misuse, should be managed through co-contributions, regional delivery, random audits and subsidy recovery. Conduct an independent evaluation at the end of year two.

Key performance indicators are:

- Training completion rates
- Number of paid placements created
- Employment, earnings and job-quality outcomes at three, six and twelve months
- Share of participants who are women and rural youth
- Retention after the subsidy ends
- Number of government AI projects completing workforce-impact assessments

## Conclusion

AI need not worsen Bhutan’s youth employment crisis. This package gives Bhutan’s National AI Strategy a measurable youth-employment dividend, not merely a technological one.
